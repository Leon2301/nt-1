\textbf{\underline{Exercise 1
Spare change}}

Imagine a world in which we have exactly two types of coin: 
one worth $P$ cents, 
one worth $Q$ cents, 
such that $\gcd(P,Q) = 1$. The goal of this
problem is to bound how many values we cannot form using these coins. 


\textbf{(i) (15pts)}
Let us first consider $P = 5, Q = 13$. Determine in how many
ways we can form 48 cents using these two coins.

\textit{
Hint: Consider the Diphantine equation $5u+13v=48$.
What is the general form of the solution? We can't have a negative number of coins!
}

\textbf{solution:} 

we must find awnsers for the following equasion.
\begin{align*}
    48 = 5u + 13v \hspace{1cm} u, v \in \bN_{0} \\
    \Rightarrow
    0 \leq u \leq 9 = \lfloor \frac{48}{5} \rfloor, 
    0 \leq v \leq 3 = \lfloor \frac{48}{13} \rfloor
\end{align*}


Now let us take Bézout's theorem and let 
the euklid algorithm run backwards. so\dots
\begin{align*}
    \gcd(13,5): \\
    13 = 2(5) + 3& \\
    5 = 1(3) + 2& \\
    3 = 1(2) + 1& 
    \Rightarrow \gcd(13,5) = 1 \\
    1 &= 3 - 1(2) \\
                 2 &= 5 - 1(3) \\
                 3 &= 13 - 2(5) \\
   \Rightarrow
    1 = 3 - 1(5- 1(3)) &= 2(3) - 1(5)\\
   2(13 - 2(5)) - 1(5) &= 2(13) - 5(5) \\
    1 = 2(13) - 5(5) \\
    \\
    48 = (2 \cdot 48)(13) + (-5 \cdot 48)(5)
        &\Rightarrow u_{0} = -240 , v_{0} = 96
\end{align*}
so we get our first solution, but it is not in our range\dots
So we just "move" the solution to find all awnsers in Range: 

\begin{align*}
    0 &\leq u \leq 9, 
    &0 \leq v \leq 3 \\
    u &= u_{0} + \frac{b}{gcd(a,b)} t , t \in \bZ
    &v = v_{0} - \frac{a}{gcd(a,b)} t , 
    t \in \bZ \\
    u &= -240 + 13 t , t \in \bZ 
    &v = 96 - 5 t , 
    t \in \bZ \\
    (t &= 19 \approx 18.46\dots = \frac{240}{13})
    &(t = 19 \approx 19.2 = \frac{96}{5})\\
    \Rightarrow
    u &= \{ 7 \}
    &\Rightarrow
    v = \{ 1 \}
\end{align*}
So we only found one valid solution.

\textbf{(ii)  (20pts)}
Show that we can form 49, 50, 51, 52 cents using coins worth
$P=5$ and $Q=13$. Here conclude that we can form any value 
$N \geq 48$.

\textbf{solution:}
\begin{align*}
    49 = 2(5) + 3(13) \\
    50 = 10(5) + 0(13) \\
    51 = 5(5) + 2(13) \\
    52 = 0(5) + 4(13) 
\end{align*}


So at first we see that every number with it's last digit be
$0$ or $5$ we can easily express through coins of $P = 5$
Therefor any number
\begin{align*}
S = \{ n \in \bN_{0} : n \%10 = 0 \lor 5 \} \\
5|s , \forall s \in S
\end{align*}
So we just need to show that $k(13) \equiv \{1,2,3,4\} \pmod{5}, k \in \bN_{0}$
while $k13 \leq 52$.
it does!
\begin{align*}
    k = 1 \Rightarrow 13 \equiv 3 \pmod{5} \\
    k = 2 \Rightarrow 26 \equiv 1 \pmod{5} \\
    k = 3 \Rightarrow 39 \equiv 4 \pmod{5} \\
    k = 4 \Rightarrow 52 \equiv 2 \pmod{5} 
\end{align*}

\textbf{(iii) (15pts)}
Can we form 47 with coins worth $P=5$ and $Q=13$?

\textbf{solution:}
no. 

$47\%5 = 2$ and we saw in (ii) that we need at least $4(13)=52$
four coins to form such a number. But 52 already overshouts 47 
and negative coins are not allowed.

\textbf{(iv) (20pts)}
Define $g(P,Q) = PQ - P -Q$.
Show that $g(P,Q)$ cannot be formed using coins worth $P$ and $Q$

\textbf{solution:}
So $\gcd(P,Q) = 1$ and I assume $P, Q \geq 1$.

\begin{align}
    PQ - P - Q &= aP + bQ , \h a,b \in \bN_{0}\\
    PQ &= (a+1)P + (b+1)Q
\end{align}
\begin{align*}    
    Q &= (a+1) + \frac{(b+1)Q}{P} 
    &P &= \frac{(a+1)P}{Q}  + (b+1)
    \\
    Q - (a+1)&= \frac{(b+1)Q}{P} 
    & P - (b+1)&= \frac{(a+1)P}{Q}
\end{align*}
and $\gcd(P,Q) = 1 \Rightarrow 
\frac{Q}{P} \notin \bN_{0} \Rightarrow
P|(b+1) \Leftrightarrow
b+1 = kP$ with $k \in \bN$

the same for $a+1 = lQ$ with $l \in \bN$

Because $a,b \geq 0$: $k,l \geq 1$. 
Combined with (2) we get:
\begin{align*}
    PQ = (lQ)P + (kP)Q = PQ(l+k) \\
    (l + k) = 1 \geq 2 ???
\end{align*}
contradiction to $k,l \geq 1$. Therefor no solution.

\textbf{(v) (20pts)}
Suppose that $P<Q$. Show that, for $N = 1, 2, ... , P$, we can write
\[
N = aP + bQ
\]
with 
\[
0 < a < Q, b+P > 0.
\]
\textit{
Hint: Start by showing we can find a solution with such an $a$
and show that corresponding $b$ satisfies the given inequality. 
Recall that $N-bQ > -bQ$
}

\textbf{solution:}
%We just use Bézout again to get our first solution and work from there on. 
\begin{align*}
    \gcd(P, Q) = 1 \\
    N = aP + bQ = 
    N(1) = (N x)P + (N y)Q \\
    1 = xP + yQ
\end{align*}
Because of Bézouts theorem there must exist an solution to this equation
let us call it $(a_{0},b_{0}) := (Nx, Ny)$.
To get all solutions we use:
\begin{align*}
    a &= a_{0} + kQ, \h k \in \bZ \\
    b &= b_{0} - lP, \h l \in \bZ
\end{align*}

% Part 1
Therefor:
\begin{align*}
    a_{0} &\equiv a \pmod{Q}
    % \\ b_{0} &\equiv b \pmod{P}
\end{align*}
So $a \in \{0,1,...,Q-1\}$. Now we need to show that $a \neq 0$: 
\begin{align*}
    aP + bQ = N \\
    aP = N - bQ
\end{align*}
If $a := 0 \Rightarrow 0 = N-bQ \Rightarrow Q | N$
That's condictionary to $1 \leq N \leq P < Q \Rightarrow a \neq 0$. 
And we get \[0<a<Q\]

% Part 2
Let's conclude: $0 < P < Q$
\begin{align*}
    ab + bQ = N \Rightarrow \h aP &= N-bQ \\
    N > 0 \Rightarrow \h N - bQ &> -bQ \\
    0<a<Q \Rightarrow \h QP &> aP \\
    \Rightarrow
    QP > aP = N-bQ &> -bQ \\
    bQ + QP &> 0 \\
    b + P &> 0
\end{align*}


\textbf{(vi) (10pts)}
Hence conclude that $g(P,Q)$ is the largest value that cannot be 
formed using coins with $P$ and $Q$.

\textbf{solution:}
So we just need to show that we can form every
$N > g(P,Q)$ with $N = aP + bQ \h a,b \in \bN_{0}$.
%If $N\geq aP$ we get:
\begin{align*}
    aP + bQ &= 1 = gcd(P,Q)\\
    \Rightarrow
    a_{1} P &\equiv 1 \pmod{Q} \\
    P^{-1} &:= a_{1} \h P^{-1} \in \bN_{0} \\
    a_{N}P &\equiv N(1) \pmod{Q} \\
    a_{N} &\equiv N P^{-1} \pmod{Q} \\
    \Rightarrow
    \exists a_{N} &\in \{0,1,\dots,Q-1\}
\end{align*}
So we have our $a$ now we need $b \in \bN_{0}$.
\begin{align*}
    aP &\equiv N \pmod{Q} 
    \Rightarrow
    Q | (N- aP)\\
    b &:= \frac{N - aP}{Q} \h b \in \bZ
    %= \frac{bQ}{Q}
\end{align*}
If $N < aP$ we get:
\begin{align*}
    N \equiv aP \pmod{Q} \\
    N \leq aP - Q
\end{align*}
We can conclude:
\begin{align*}
    a \in \{0,1,\dots,Q-1\}
    \Rightarrow a \leq Q-1 \\
    g(P,Q) = PQ - P - Q = (Q-1)P - Q
    \geq aP - Q \geq N
\end{align*}
So every $N$ without a solution must be $N \leq g(P,Q)$
that let us conclude: 
\[N>g(P,Q)\] 
always has a solution for $N=aP+bQ$ in $\bN_{0}$.
